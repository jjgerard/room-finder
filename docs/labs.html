One room

Eight computer labs carry CEBE in their names, six of them School of Computing. Eight schools teach in them. Seven could be reserved for Computing tomorrow and nobody would notice. The eighth decides whether Spring 2026 has a legal timetable at all.

The eight rooms

Could use it counts classes that do not already teach in a CEBE lab but could use this one. Sole option counts those for which it is the only CEBE room that fits.

Why one of them carries the set

What closing them costs

Three arms, twenty random starting points each, on Spring 2026. A class may use a CEBE lab only if it already teaches in one; everything else is identical between arms, so the difference is the room rule and nothing else.

The ask is one room, not eight. BC-03-311 is the only computer lab on campus holding more than 50. Seven of the eight could be reserved for the School of Computing with no effect on any other school; the eighth cannot, and in the tighter of the two terms it is the difference between a timetable that breaks no rules and one that does.

How this was checked

Two independent methods agree. The room figures above are counted directly from the timetable — no search, no randomness, and they regenerate from the model the solver uses. The arms are sixty separate runs of that solver. Neither depends on the other, and a fault in one would not produce the same answer in the other.

The arms cannot cross. Releasing a room only ever adds options, so a looser fence can never score worse than a tighter one. That holds here on every measure — best, median and mean — which is what distinguishes a real effect from search noise, and it is checked by a test rather than by eye. Four of the twenty seeds individually did better under the tighter fence, which is why the arms are compared in aggregate and not one run at a time.

What would falsify it. A computer lab above 50 seats that the inventory does not list; a class in the 58–90 band that can in fact use a room the model rules out; or Spring reaching zero from any of the twenty starting points with all eight closed. None of the three is ruled out by argument — they are simply not what the data shows.

Three limits, stated plainly
  • Nobody is stranded. No class that loses its only CEBE option is left with nowhere at all — each has rooms outside the set. The cost is lost flexibility, not impossibility.
  • Spring has little slack to begin with. It reaches a legal timetable from 6 of 30 starting points against Autumn's 9, and carries 3,302 bookings against 2,369. Autumn absorbs the same closure without becoming unsolvable. The claim holds for the tighter term, not for both.
  • Which rooms a class may use is partly inferred. 98% of class sizes in the model come from the room the class currently sits in rather than a confirmed figure, so “rooms available” is the pool the model considers eligible, not a survey of what would physically work.
